# Copy frameworks for LinkedIn ads

Read this when choosing a structure for an ad, editing a draft, or diagnosing why copy underperforms. Frameworks are tools: pick the one that fits the angle, don't stack all of them.

## Structure formulas

**AIDA** (E. St. Elmo Lewis). Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. Hook, a relevant detail or stat, the outcome painted vividly, a clear CTA. Best for short ads, Most-Aware audiences, and headline-plus-CTA pairs.

**PAS** (Problem, Agitate, Solve). Name a specific, recognizable pain. Twist the knife with the cost of inaction. Offer the resolution. The natural home of the cost-of-inaction angle. Best for single-image ads aimed at Problem-Aware readers.

**BAB** (Before, After, Bridge). Current frustrating reality, a vivid better state, then the product as the bridge. Good for case-study ads, carousels, and Unaware readers because it starts from their world rather than the product.

**PASTOR** (Ray Edwards). Problem, Amplify, Story/Solution, Transformation/Testimony, Offer, Response. Needs room. Use for carousels, long-form posts, and the landing page that follows the ad, not a 600-character intro.

**QUEST** (Perry Marshall). Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition. For cold or skeptical audiences. Qualify early ("For ops leaders at 200+ person logistics companies..."), show you understand their world, explain why the old way fails, create desire for the new way, hand off to a low-friction next step. Strong for top-of-funnel Solution-Aware angles.

**StoryBrand SB7** (Donald Miller). Customer is the character with a problem, meets a guide (the brand), gets a plan, is called to act, avoids failure, reaches success. Use as a sanity check on any ad: is the reader the hero, and does the brand supply a simple plan?

## Editing checklists

**4 Cs** (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers). Run after drafting.
- Clear: is the point immediate?
- Concise: does every word earn its place?
- Compelling: is there tension or stakes?
- Credible: is every claim backed by data, a quote, or a logo?

**4 Us** (AWAI). Score hook candidates 1-4 on Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific. In B2B, specificity (real numbers, named outcomes) is usually the biggest lever. Prefer "9 hours a week" to "a lot of time".

**FAB ladder** (Features, Advantages, Benefits). For any feature you're tempted to lead with, ask "so what?" twice until you reach the buyer-level benefit: time, money, status, or reduced risk. Lead with the benefit.

**B2B Message Layers** (Wynter). Buyers clear four layers in strict order: Clarity ("What is it?"), Relevance ("Is it for me?"), Value ("Do I want it?"), Differentiation ("Why you?"). They don't care about value until they know what it is. When an ad underperforms, find the first layer that fails. A hook with a great value claim and no hint of what the product is fails at layer one.

## Audience and offer diagnostics

**Five levels of awareness** (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*). The same angle won't work across all five.
- Unaware: pattern-interrupt story, a hook about their situation rather than a product
- Problem-Aware: agitate the problem, show the cost
- Solution-Aware: prove the mechanism, show why the usual approach falls short
- Product-Aware: differentiate against alternatives
- Most Aware: the offer and a low-friction CTA

Use awareness as a source of genuinely different premises when building the angle set.

**Value Equation** (Alex Hormozi). Perceived value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood) / (Time Delay x Effort). Copy moves these levers: make the outcome specific, add proof to raise likelihood, compress perceived time-to-value and effort ("set up in 15 minutes"). Useful for offer-led and retargeting ads.

**Positioning** (April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*). Five components: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, the value those enable, target customers who care most, and the market category. Do this before writing angles, so angles come from real differentiation rather than guesses. If the user can't state what buyers would use instead of the product, positioning is the first job.

## Creative principles

**Clarity over clever** (Anthony Pierri, Dave Rigotti, Fletch PMM). Buyers bounce when they can't tell what the product is, who it's for, and how it differs. Lead with category, outcome, and who it's for. Save wit for the supporting line.

**Demand creation vs. capture** (Chris Walker, Refine Labs). Most B2B paid social underperforms because it looks like an ad and pitches a demo too early. Separate *create demand* creative (educational, point-of-view, founder-voiced, native to the feed) from *capture demand* creative (direct offer, for people already aware). Don't ask cold audiences for a demo.

**Ads that don't look like ads** (Dave Gerhardt, Exit Five). Plain-text, first-person, real photo, written like a post a person would write, tends to beat polished corporate ad units because it avoids ad-blindness. Practical rules: first person, no stock photography, a real person's voice and photo, post structure instead of banner structure.

## Premise menu with quick examples

Illustrative only. Replace with the user's real claims and numbers.

| Premise | Shape of the claim | Example hook |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of inaction | What the status quo quietly costs | "9 hours a week. That's what manual planning quietly costs each person." |
| Contrarian take | The accepted wisdom is wrong | "More headcount won't fix your forecasting." |
| Status-quo critique | The current tool or process is the problem | "Your planning spreadsheet was never meant to do this." |
| Quantified outcome | A specific result | "[X]% fewer stockouts in [Y] weeks." |
| Identity / status | What a person like the reader does | "The ops leaders hitting plan don't rebuild forecasts on Fridays." |
| Risk reduction | What can go wrong and how it's removed | "One bad forecast can cost a quarter. Here's the audit trail." |
| Speed | Time to value | "Live in 15 minutes, not 15 weeks." |
| Peer / social proof | Others like you already did | "[Peer type] cut planning time by half. Here's how." |
| Problem reframing | The problem isn't what you think | "Your ops team isn't slow. Your planning process is." |
